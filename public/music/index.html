DJ Ruckus · David Ruck · Christchurch, New Zealand

From a choir boy to DJ Ruckus: the sounds that made me.

Before there was Nirvana, before I played drums, before I found underground electronic music and called myself DJ Ruckus, there was God, a choir, my mother Pauline, and a new record every week or two. That is where my lifelong fascination with sound really began.

A personal music memoir · the earliest influences

God gave me a foundation before I knew where the music would take me.

There are very few people or artists who have come into my life and left an impression deep enough to shape my actual persona. That is a different thing from enjoying a song. I am talking about the sounds that become part of how you think, feel, remember and eventually understand yourself.

When I was very young, I was a choir boy. I joined the Treble Boys’ Choir at about four while attending St Andrew’s College in Christchurch. Looking back, I believe God was directing me even then. He gave me a musical foundation through hymns such as All Things Bright and Beautiful, through melody, voices, harmony and the experience of singing as part of something larger than myself.

I cannot pretend that a child understands the significance of all those experiences while they are happening. I certainly did not know where they would lead. But I now see that foundation as intentional. It taught me to hear music as something with meaning before I had any idea about genres, sound engineering or the records I would later spend so much of my life chasing.

My mother Pauline, the record shop and a new vinyl every week or two.

Then there was my mother, Pauline. She grew me up on vinyl: Pink Floyd, Dire Straits, Genesis, Fleetwood Mac, Billy Joel and Elton John. Long before I was making my own decisions about what to listen to, those records were teaching me that music could be expansive, intricate, intimate, theatrical and completely different from one side of an album to the other.

But the music is inseparable from the memory of how it came into our home. My mother ran a T-shirt and clothing manufacturing and printing business. She worked for her money. I remember that she could be working until midnight, sometimes into the early hours of the morning, to get an order finished.

And then, with her hard-earned money from the week, she would throw me in the car and take me excitedly down to the local record shop. Buying a new record, once every week or two, was her treat. That excitement was infectious. The record was not just something to put on in the background. It was something worth going out for, something we could bring home and discover.

I do not want that part of the story reduced to a list of famous musicians. I remember the effort behind those records. The late nights. The business. The pride and pleasure of being able to choose something new. It was an early lesson in what music can mean when it has been earned, sought out and shared.

When I later spent endless hours searching for unusual tracks as a DJ, I was doing my own version of something I had witnessed as a child. The formats changed, the music changed and the places I looked changed. The excitement of finding the next extraordinary sound did not.

Then came the cassette with no name. I was twelve.

By then, I had been given a formidable musical foundation. But around the age of twelve, a very different sound arrived that felt like the beginning of my own direction.

Someone I knew handed me a cassette tape they had found in the gutter. Whatever had once been written on it had worn away. There was no band name, no album title and no useful clue about what I was about to hear.

I took it home and played it. The reaction was immediate: this was the sound I had been thirsting for. I wanted more of it badly, but I did not even know who I was looking for.

Years later I finally discovered that the cassette was Nirvana’s Bleach. Kurt Cobain and Nirvana became defining influences of my teenage years. But I see that tape as more than the discovery of one band. It opened my own mental amusement park, a wonderland of sounds and imagined landscapes that I have kept exploring for a lifetime.

Pauline had opened the door to the possibilities of music. The cassette made me want to find my own way through them.

Before search boxes

You could not just sit down and find the answer.

There was no YouTube, Spotify, Shazam or search engine waiting to identify a half-destroyed cassette. If you wanted something, you had to go out into the world. Ask people. Visit record shops. Swap tapes. Read flyers. Talk to DJs. Find the person who knew the person who knew the track.

I was internally much more awkward than I probably appeared. I learned to push that aside and approach people anyway. That mattered far beyond music. Years later the same behaviour showed up in event promotion, sales, radio, business development and almost everything else I built: if I wanted an outcome, I learned not to wait for it to arrive.

From choir to drums, and from drums to bands.

At around twelve I left Christchurch for boarding school at Timaru Boys’ High School, started drumming and became much more interested in rock. The choir foundation did not disappear. It simply found a very different outlet.

I wanted a band badly. The problem was not wanting to make music. It was finding two or three other people who were equally motivated, rehearsed and actually going to turn up. I spent plenty of time trying, but that dependency frustrated me.

DJing solved a problem.

DJing meant I could still build an entire musical experience without waiting for the rest of a band. I could hunt for records, decide exactly what I wanted to play, prepare it and put it in front of people.

My taste was never confined to one lane. I was heavily influenced by 1980s hip hop and later became most naturally associated with a harder edge of electro and house. I also loved selected drum & bass that felt genuinely engineered rather than generic, including the harder territory around Panacea, Metalheadz and Usual Suspects. When dubstep emerged, the darker and heavier end of that interested me for the same reason.

What I believe makes a DJ

The art is in the hours nobody sees.

In my opinion, what separates a great DJ from an average one has very little to do with what happens in front of a mirror before a gig. It happens when nobody is watching: in the hours spent listening, rejecting, comparing, rewinding, and searching for something that stops you in your tracks.

Imagine being willing to dig through hundreds of hours of tracks in a week just to find ten minutes of genuinely exciting new music, a sound or a combination that actually deserves a place in your set. To some people that would sound absurd. To me, that is the kind of passion the craft demands at its highest level.

It is not about finding ten minutes that are merely new. It is about finding ten minutes that are worth hearing. There is an enormous difference.

Finding a record is not the same as finding a sound.

Some records are functional. They have a place, they fill a gap, they keep things moving. There is nothing inherently wrong with that. But a record being serviceable is not the same as a record being extraordinary. An exciting sound might last only a few bars; an astonishing track might be buried in a release hardly anybody noticed.

When I talk about digging, I mean listening past the convenient categories and the obvious recommendations. I mean recognising an unusual texture, an impossible-sounding rhythm, a bassline with character, a transition that should not work but somehow does. I mean having enough reference points in your head to hear when somebody has done something truly different.

Then comes the other part: selection. Knowing when to play it, what to put before it, what to leave out and whether the room is ready. A rare record in the hands of somebody who does not understand it is still just a record. A great DJ can make its arrival feel inevitable.

My taste has always crossed genres. The label on the sleeve matters less to me than the imagination, intention and skill I can hear in the work. I do not need to love an entire genre to love one extraordinary piece of music within it.

Noisia: the electronic standard I keep coming back to.

I have many musical influences, but none has shaped my appreciation of electronic music quite like Noisia. What draws me in is not simply intensity, tempo or how hard something hits. It is the precision and the invention. The sense that every sound has been considered, sculpted, pushed and placed with extraordinary care.

Drum & bass, at its best, can do something that not many forms of music manage: combine near-physical force with incredible intricacy. Noisia demonstrated to me how far that can go. A bass sound does not have to be just a bass sound. Percussion can become architecture. Silence can carry as much intent as an impact. A mix can be brutally powerful without becoming a shapeless wall of noise.

That is why, when I say I admire electronic music, I am not talking about a beat and a drop arranged around a predictable formula. I am talking about the imagination and engineering underneath the surface. Noisia are a benchmark for that kind of attention to sound. I can admire the construction even while thinking about how it would actually translate through a sound system in front of people.

It is also why so much electronic music can leave me cold. Technology has made it possible to create and distribute more music than ever. It has not made the rare moments of genuine originality any less rare.

Igorrr: when one or two tracks become entire albums.

There are very few artists in my life who make an impression so strong that I want to play a track repeatedly, let alone several tracks, let alone sit with an entire album and then come back to it. Igorrr belongs in that exceptionally small category.

Part of what I value in Igorrr is the refusal to behave like one musical genre owes allegiance to another. Electronic programming, extreme metal, classical and baroque ideas, extraordinary vocal contrasts and apparently incompatible rhythms can occupy the same piece without the result feeling like a cheap novelty. It can be chaotic, funny, violent, beautiful and meticulously controlled, sometimes in the space of seconds.

That takes a musical imagination I find genuinely rare. Plenty of people can make something strange. Far fewer can make the strange feel deliberate, compelling and replayable. With Igorrr, I hear an artist willing to take enormous creative risks and still make the result feel like it belongs to a recognisable musical world.

What matters most to me is the repeat-listening test. When the initial shock has worn off, do I still want to hear it? Is there something else in the arrangement, the recording or the detail to discover on the tenth listen? With Igorrr, my answer is yes, across far more than a token standout track or two.

The DJ became the spectacle. I still care about the records.

I think a great deal of mainstream DJ culture, including mainstream EDM, has become more concerned with how the DJ appears than what the DJ has actually found. The outfit, the poses, the social-media clips, the image of the person behind the decks: all of it can overshadow the reason the decks were there in the first place.

There is nothing wrong with performance, personality or presentation. A good show matters. But when the packaging becomes the product, the music can start to feel disposable. I find that disappointing because the less visible side of DJing, the love of records and the compulsion to search, is where I think the most interesting talent lives.

Music has always had its throwaway material, not just electronic music. Most genres have far more ordinary releases than life-changing ones. What has changed is the speed and scale at which platforms can propel the ordinary into everybody's attention. An average record can be made to seem culturally unavoidable without becoming any more interesting to hear.

I do not think popularity is proof of quality, and I do not think obscurity guarantees it either. Sometimes a huge record really is brilliant. Sometimes a forgotten one is forgotten for a reason. The job, to my mind, is to listen well enough to tell the difference for yourself.

Algorithms can recommend. They cannot do the listening for me.

I am not against technology. I have spent a great deal of my working life building with it. Discovery tools can open doors I never would have found in the old days, and I use that kind of possibility with real appreciation. But an algorithm presenting a track to me is not the same thing as my hearing what makes it remarkable.

However sophisticated the recommendation becomes, taste still calls for judgment, patience and context. The important question is not merely whether a system has found something statistically similar to music I already enjoy. It is whether the music has something to say, whether the production reveals real thought and whether it survives another listen after the novelty is gone.

That last part matters enormously. The greatest discoveries do not always announce themselves in the first thirty seconds. Some take time to unfold. Others sound so far removed from what you thought you liked that the recommendation would probably never have put them in front of you.

Beyond the track

The record, the speaker and the room are one experience.

There is another layer to all of this that people too often overlook. A beautifully engineered piece of music is only the beginning. The loudspeaker that reproduces it and the room or environment into which it is projected are part of the experience. You cannot fully separate them.

It is possible to have a wonderful recording and hear a poor version of it because of the playback system. It is possible to have expensive speakers in an unsuitable room and still miss what was put into the music. The placement, the acoustics, the reflections, the balance, the dynamics and the way low frequencies behave can turn the same track into something sublime or something exhausting.

That is part of why I find sound engineering so fascinating. The most impressive experience is not necessarily the loudest one. It is the moment the detail, force, space and emotional intention of a recording are allowed to arrive together, without one destroying the others.

Some people hear what others never notice.

I believe there is an art to making sound, an art to reproducing it and an art to knowing how a space will receive it. The physics can be studied. Techniques can be taught. Technology can help enormously. Yet the highest level of sensitivity, imagination and instinct still strikes me as something extraordinarily rare.

For me, that gift ultimately comes from God. I do not say that to dismiss years of practice, the work of engineers or the painstaking craft behind a great system. Quite the opposite. I believe the gift obliges its holder to develop it. The greatest talent is neither an excuse to stop learning nor a shortcut around dedication.

When the composition, the engineering, the speaker and the environment come together immaculately, there is a kind of beauty that cannot be reduced to a playlist, a follower count or an algorithm. You feel the difference. And once you have heard it, you spend a remarkable amount of your life looking for it again.

Production: the work I was least willing to release.

I was always interested in production as well as DJing, but I was extremely hard on anything I made. Most tracks stayed with me because I could hear every flaw and usually decided they were not good enough.

One exception began as a technical exercise. I was practising time-stretching a vocal over a completely different genre and built a drum & bass remix around an Eminem vocal. It was not even especially representative of my main DJ sound.

The audience disagreed with my assessment. The remix accumulated hundreds of thousands of listens on SoundCloud in my recollection, which eventually convinced me to put it on YouTube. That upload has since passed 35,000 views.

Music taught me to move.

The part of this story that matters most to me is not genre trivia. I grew up before discovery became frictionless. Finding the right record, finding musicians, finding a venue, finding a crowd or getting a flyer into the right hands required movement.

I was not naturally fearless. I learned to act while feeling awkward. I learned to walk up to strangers, ask questions, hand people things, promote ideas and tolerate rejection because there was no digital shortcut around doing it.

That habit eventually became one of the most useful skills of my working life.
