David Ruck · DJ Ruckus · music and the people behind it

The music that made me.

My earliest memories of music are about the people who brought it into my life: a childhood choir, my mother Pauline and her weekly trips to the record shop, a cousin in Bournemouth and a DJ with two Technics turntables.

Then came a nameless Nirvana cassette, the few artists who opened up whole new worlds, and eventually my own need to put records on and make a room come alive. This is how I remember it, and why I am still listening.

01 / FOUNDATIONS

Music was part of my life
before I understood it.

When I look at the handful of people and artists who have genuinely shaped my persona, I have to start long before I ever heard a guitar amplifier or stood behind a pair of turntables. I was a choir boy. At about four, while attending St Andrew's College in Christchurch, I joined the Treble Boys' Choir.

Looking back, I believe God was preparing me. Hymns such as All Things Bright and Beautiful taught me melody, harmony and the curious feeling of voices becoming something larger than the people singing them. I did not understand how important that foundation would become. I do now.

But it was my mother, Pauline, who made music part of everyday life. She raised me on vinyl: Pink Floyd, Dire Straits, Genesis, Fleetwood Mac, Billy Joel and Elton John. In that house, music had colour, drama, patience and imagination. One record could sound like an enormous landscape. Another could feel like somebody was sitting beside you telling a story.

What stays with me most is not just the records. Pauline ran a T-shirt and clothing manufacturing and printing business. She worked for her money, sometimes until midnight or into the early morning to finish an order. Then, once every week or two, she would throw me in the car and drive excitedly to the local record shop to spend some of what she had earned on a new vinyl record.

It was her treat. I got to be there. I watched somebody who had worked so hard for her money take genuine joy in choosing a piece of music to bring home. Long before I understood record digging, she had shown me why a new record could matter.

“The record was never just another thing to own. It was something worth working for, going out to find, and taking home to discover.”

02 / TWO DOORS AT TWELVE

Two moments I have
never forgotten.

At about twelve, two experiences opened different parts of my musical world. One happened while I was visiting family in England. The other involved a cassette tape so anonymous that I had no way of finding out who had made the music on it.

Simon, Martyn and two Technics 1200s.

My mother took me to England to meet family. In Bournemouth, my cousin Simon, who was about eighteen, took me to my first house party. His friend Martyn was DJing. He had two Technics 1200 turntables and a pile of vinyl, and the whole arrangement looked to me like the control centre of a spaceship.

The record I remember most clearly from that night is The Prodigy's Charly, the one I knew as “Charlie Says”. I remember it feeling freshly released, completely alien to my ears and unlike anything I'd encountered in New Zealand. I wasn't just hearing a new style of music. I was watching Martyn mix vinyl and turn a room full of people into one shared experience.

I was captivated by the equipment as much as the music: two turntables, a mixer, Martyn choosing the records, and the room responding. At twelve I did not know what to do with what I had seen. I did know I would never forget it.

The unmarked tape.

Someone I knew handed me a cassette that had been found in the gutter. Whatever had been written on it had worn away. No artist name. No album. No way to ask a search engine what it was.

I took it home and pressed play. This was the sound I had been thirsting for. It felt like somebody had opened a door directly into my own imagination. I was desperate to hear more, but I did not even know whose music I was listening to.

Years later I discovered it was Nirvana's Bleach. Kurt Cobain became an enormous influence, but more importantly, that unknown cassette had given me my own direction. Pauline had taught me how much music could contain. Nirvana made me feel there might be an entire, untamed landscape inside my own head.

03 / BANDS AND BREAKTHROUGHS

The bands that changed
what I wanted to play.

I could spend what felt like an endless amount of time indulging my ears, hunting for the next sound. But I was particular, and I still am. Finding a song I liked was one thing. Finding an artist who could make me accept a new genre was something else entirely.

Rage Against the Machine was one of those exceptions. Killing in the Name felt like a new musical language. The guitar, the rhythm, the force and the restraint of the parts coming together had an effect on what seemed like every school band around me, including my own. We all wanted to make that sound.

Those formative years included Kaiapoi High School. Towards the end of my time there, I was mostly turning up for the subjects that mattered to me, English and music. I had left school by fourteen and begun working in hospitality, initially in kitchens. It wasn't a tidy transition from school into a musical career. It was life, work and making things happen wherever I could.

I wanted to be in bands, but the difficulty was never a lack of desire to play. It was finding two or three other people who were prepared to show up, practise, agree and keep going. The dependence on everybody else could be maddening.

Big Day Out, 1996: Pauline, a stranger and Weezer.

About this time I'd become friends with two older girls who were regulars at Ministry nightclub in Christchurch: Lynette and Pauline. Lynette was a gorgeous blonde who worked at Wendy's ice cream parlour, where she had to wear a ridiculously cute pink outfit. I had a complete crush on her. But it was Pauline, my friend rather than my mother, who offered to take me on an adventure I have never forgotten.

She invited me to drive to Auckland for the Big Day Out and paid for the entire trip. Rage Against the Machine were on the bill. For somebody who had been so affected by Killing in the Name, the thought of seeing them live was almost unbelievable.

Somehow Pauline and I became separated in Auckland before the festival even began. I was on my own when I met a young woman in the street. I can't tell you her name now, but I remember her kindness, and I remember what happened next. She took me back to the place where she was living and asked if I wanted to listen to Weezer.

“Who's that?” I asked.

She seemed genuinely shocked. “Oh my God, you've never heard of Weezer?” She put on the Blue Album, and I was sold almost immediately. Say It Ain't So in particular went straight in. Another door had opened, and it had happened in the most unexpected way.

As we talked, I learned that her parents had died and that she lived with her older sister. She offered to let me stay the night. She was going to the Big Day Out too, and reasoned that she could take me along in the morning. It was a brief encounter, but an incredibly poignant one. I fell in love with her that day.

We went to the concert together. At the front gate she spotted her friends, went to join them, and that was the last time I ever saw her. No grand goodbye, no reunion. Just a person who appeared in my life for a moment, gave me a record that has stayed with me for decades, and disappeared again.

Coincidentally, Pauline was standing at the gate too, looking less than impressed. “Where have you BEEN!?” she demanded. We walked in together, but I spent the day on my own. And I did what I had travelled all that way to do: I made it to the front row for Rage Against the Machine. I can still remember how extraordinary it felt.

Pauline and I drove back to Christchurch together, then went our separate ways. I'll never forget her generosity or the trip she made possible. And I'll never forget the girl in Auckland. To this day, Weezer, especially Say It Ain't So, takes me straight back to her room, to the Blue Album and to those few days that stand out so clearly in my life.

Auckland's Big Day Out was held on 19 January 1996, at Ericsson Stadium (Mount Smart). The date and Rage Against the Machine's appearance are documented in the NZ History festival archive ↗. The rest is my own recollection.

Nelson, a bandmate, and Pantera.

At around eighteen I was living in Nelson for the first time, working at The Boat Shed Cafe. The guitarist in my band introduced me to Pantera's Far Beyond Driven, especially I'm Broken. I can still remember the sensation of hearing it: the metal and hard rock genres had mostly remained closed to me, and now somebody had blown the lid clean off.

Suddenly there was another entire menu to explore. Yet I was no less fussy. Heaviness on its own didn't impress me. What struck me was the command of the players and the apparently exact understanding of the sound they wanted.

Then This Love hit me like a sledgehammer, right at the soul. It carried vulnerability and aggression in the same breath, a kind of emotional violence that went far deeper than simply being loud. The two songs did different things to me: I'm Broken opened the genre; This Love broke through emotionally.

I'm Broken is from Far Beyond Driven (1994); This Love is from Vulgar Display of Power (1992). Their position here follows my own discoveries, not album release order.

04 / DJ RUCKUS

I wanted to play music.
DJing gave me a way.

It was around that period in Nelson, as Pantera was widening my appreciation of heavy music, that I began DJing. I was becoming desperate to play sound, to share the things that were happening inside my head, and I was beginning to understand just how difficult it could be to hold a band together.

DJing and throwing elaborate parties at my house came to the rescue. I didn't need to wait for a drummer, a guitarist and a singer all to agree on what we were doing. I could find the records, put the equipment together, create the atmosphere, invite people and make the night happen.

It was a return to the magic of Martyn's setup in Bournemouth. Back then I had been the twelve-year-old staring at a spaceship. Now I wanted to operate the thing. The turntables were a way to build an entire experience with my own hands, and there was something liberating about that.

My taste never belonged to one narrow genre. The 1980s hip-hop I loved, harder electro and house, selected drum & bass around Panacea, Metalheadz and Usual Suspects, and later the more interesting edges of dubstep all had a place. I wasn't trying to join every new trend. I was trying to find what was distinctive within it.

Finding the right ten minutes.

As a DJ I have always thought the real work happens long before a set, while nobody is looking. I would rather spend hundreds of hours searching through music to find ten minutes worth playing than spend those hours making myself look the part. For me, the art is selection, not the costume.

And selection is not simply picking a rare record. It is knowing why it is special, when it belongs, what should come before it and what the room can take. Sometimes a few bars of sound are enough to transform the energy. Sometimes the most disciplined decision is to leave a track out.

“Hundreds of hours of music to find ten minutes that are actually worth hearing.”

05 / THE RARE ONES

The rare artists I can
listen to again and again.

I have many influences, but when it comes to electronic music, Noisia stands above the rest for me. Not because their records are simply hard or fast, but because they sound constructed with almost impossible care. The bass has character. The percussion feels designed rather than selected. Space, silence, attack and detail are all decisions.

They remind me that electronic music is not merely arranging beats and waiting for a drop. At its highest level it is sound engineering as a creative language. A record can have astonishing physical force and still be intricate enough to reward another listen, and another.

Igorrr: an entire album is a rarer achievement still.

Most artists might produce one or two tracks that I feel compelled to replay. Very few can sustain that fascination across multiple songs, let alone an album or two. Igorrr crosses that threshold. Electronic programming, metal, classical ideas, the human voice and apparently incompatible rhythms come together in music that can be grotesque, beautiful, funny, devastating and precise.

I admire the depth of ability, understanding and desire required to pursue such a particular sound. It is one thing to want something strange. It is another to hear it vividly enough that you are willing to do all the work required to make it real. 06 / THE ART OF LISTENING

The record is only
part of the experience.

There is a side of DJ culture I have become uncomfortable with. In much of the mainstream, including mainstream EDM, the clothes, the poses and the social-media performance can overshadow the music itself. So much ordinary material is propelled into view that the public face of DJing risks becoming more about being seen than about listening.

I don't believe every obscure record is brilliant or every popular one is worthless. The good ones still exist in both places. But nobody can outsource their ears. An algorithm might find something I haven't heard, and I value that enormously, yet it cannot decide whether a sound deserves a place in my life. That judgment still comes from listening, and often from listening repeatedly.

The speaker and the room are also instruments.

Even a perfectly engineered piece of music can be lost through the wrong playback system. A loudspeaker is not an invisible delivery service. The room isn't neutral. The acoustics, reflections, placement, dynamics, bass behaviour and the relationship of every listener to the system can change what arrives in their ears.

When a remarkable recording, a carefully built sound system and the right environment come together, something happens that cannot be described by a stream count or a recommendation score. I have enormous respect for those who understand and can shape that experience.

I believe that extraordinary sensitivity is a gift from God. That doesn't diminish the years of practice or meticulous engineering. If anything, it makes the work more important. A gift is something to develop, not a reason to stop learning.

“The recording, the loudspeaker and the room are one experience. The rarest talent hears how they belong together.”
07 / MY OWN MUSIC

Why sharing my own
music is so difficult.

Why do I feel embarrassed to release my own music? Because there is an almost endless supply of true magic already created by other people. When I listen to what they have achieved, I hear both the possibility of sound and every part of my own work that doesn't quite measure up to what I know can be done.

That is not a comfortable position for somebody who wants to make records. The same deep admiration that makes me create can make it feel presumptuous to put anything of my own into the world. I can hear the unfinished details, the things I would change and the opportunities I might have missed.

But the listener may hear something else entirely. A drum & bass remix I began as a technical exercise, time-stretching an Eminem vocal into a completely different genre, was something I nearly kept to myself. It wasn't even particularly representative of the music I usually DJed. Yet, from memory, it received hundreds of thousands of SoundCloud listens, and the YouTube upload later passed 35,000 views.

I still hear its imperfections. Other people heard something they wanted to play again. There is a lesson there that I am still trying to accept: maybe a piece of music doesn't have to compete with every remarkable thing I have ever heard before I allow somebody else to enjoy it.

Some things never stopped being about the search.

From Pauline taking me to the record shop, to Simon and Martyn's house party in Bournemouth, to Nirvana, Rage, Pantera, my own house parties, radio and production, it has always been the same obsession expressed in different forms.

Find the sound. Understand why it matters. Build the best experience you can around it. And if something truly extraordinary comes along, give it the attention it deserves.
