Career · Hospitality · c. 1991–1998

Hospitality: learning standards before I learned sales.

Before call centres, telecoms and business systems, I spent years in South Island kitchens. I generally worked at each restaurant for about a year, often a little longer. That was long enough to absorb the pace, preparation, consistency and discipline of serious service rather than simply pass through it.

The pattern

I kept landing in high-standard kitchens.

These were not random hospitality jobs. My recollection of the period is that every restaurant I worked in was either carrying Restaurant of the Year honours, on a multi-year award run, or operating at that level while I was there.

Thirty years later, the online record is uneven. Some venues no longer exist and much of the evidence survives only in old newspaper indexes, hospitality archives and photographs. What remains is still enough to show the calibre of the places: Le Bon Bolli was named New Zealand Restaurant of the Year in 1997; the Boat Shed had a documented string of national cuisine awards; Mansfield House is recorded as an award-winning restaurant and bar; Parkroyal was Christchurch’s first five-star hotel and housed nationally recognised dining; and Miyazu went on to a long-running award record in Nelson.

What the kitchen taught me

Standards are operational.

You can have talent, but if the prep is wrong, timing slips, communication breaks or one station stops performing, the whole service suffers. Years later I recognised the same thing in sales teams, call centres and technology businesses.

Venues

The restaurants and hotel kitchens.

Roughly a year, usually a little more, in each place. Some survive under the same name, some evolved, and some now exist mainly in archives.

Christchurch · hotel kitchen

Parkroyal Hotel

The Parkroyal on Victoria Square was Christchurch’s first five-star hotel. Its hospitality operation included multiple restaurants and bars, including Canterbury Tales and Yamagen, and the hotel archives preserve photographs of the kitchens, chefs and dining rooms.

The hotel was a substantial part of the Christchurch hospitality landscape I entered as a young chef. The building later became the Crowne Plaza and was demolished after the Canterbury earthquakes.

The Boat Shed was already a serious waterfront restaurant when I worked in Nelson. Historical listings describe it as one of Nelson’s leading seafood restaurants, with a string of national cuisine awards. It remains on Wakefield Quay today.

I am continuing to add older newspaper and photographic references as they surface. The purpose is not to retrofit prestige onto old jobs. It is to preserve the context: I learned hospitality inside unusually demanding kitchens, and that standard stayed with me.

The transferable lesson

Preparation creates performance.

Mise en place, timing, repetition, station discipline and service recovery are systems. That idea followed me straight into telephone sales, staff training, call-centre operations and eventually technology infrastructure.

Why this belongs in my career story

I learned pressure before I learned management.

A busy kitchen gives immediate feedback. You cannot hide a broken process behind a meeting or a report. Either the service works, or it does not. That shaped the way I later looked at every operation I built.
