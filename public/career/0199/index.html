0199: turning sales ability into infrastructure.

0199 combined a four-digit number, business data, a VOIP call centre, outbound sales and inbound directory assistance. The real story began with two separate tracks: my mother Pauline Ruck proving a directory concept from a tiny home office, while I was learning how to build aggressive, repeatable sales systems in high-performance call-centre environments.

The sales system came first

My call-centre career did not begin with 0199. Hotel Dynamics Group taught me scripting, qualification and objection handling, but the most valuable lesson was the close.

“Visa, Amex or Mastercard?”

The first close was not supposed to end the conversation. It was designed to force the real objection into the open.

Close → objection → handle it → close again.

“Email me.” “I need to talk to my wife.” “Call me next week.” Price. Timing. Trust. Most objections were familiar and had a response. You handled the objection and went straight back to the same confident close: “Visa, Amex or Mastercard?”

Then you shut up. No rescuing the customer from the silence. They either gave you the card digits, produced another objection, or hung up. If another objection came, you handled it and closed again.

That was the HDG discipline I carried forward. The close was not a polite question saved for the end. It was a diagnostic tool that exposed what was actually stopping the sale, one objection at a time.

I then took that framework into TelstraClear and proved to myself that an entrenched sales-channel assumption could be wrong. A properly designed telephone process could sell products the organisation believed required expensive face-to-face selling.

The lesson I carried forward: sales performance is not just individual talent. It can be turned into scripts, training, process, data and infrastructure.

One Number: my mother proved the concept before I saw the scale

At almost the same time I was establishing my career at TelstraClear, my mother, Pauline Ruck, was running a small operation called One Number from a tiny home office.

She did not come from the high-performance call-centre and sales environments I did. She contracted an outside call centre to answer inbound calls, had a very small website, ran some radio advertising and sold the service to local tradespeople for about $1,500. I even voiced some of the radio ads for her.

A lot of the sale was Pauline herself. The tradespeople liked and trusted her. She had already proved that businesses would pay serious money for the concept, but she was not building the kind of industrial sales machine I was learning to build.

She had the concept. I eventually saw the machine.

When I finally understood what she was actually doing, I put two and two together: directory data + one memorable number + a call centre + trained closers + objection handling + card payments.

That was the moment I realised this did not have to stay a small home-office business. With the sales systems I was learning, it could become something much bigger.

By the time it clicked for me, Pauline had already sold One Number

Pauline had already taken the concept onto national television on Dragons’ Den. The Dragons did not invest, but the appearance matters historically because it predates my later involvement with the Christchurch directory venture.

By the time I properly grasped the scale of the opportunity, she had already sold One Number for about $50,000 to a business acquisitions manager acting for an investor.

Later, while I was looking for backing for my own version of the idea, I happened to meet that investor. As I explained the concept he stopped me and asked, “What’s your name again? I feel like I’ve heard this somewhere before.” I said, “David Ruck.” He replied, “Oh wow, Pauline Ruck?” I said, “Yes, that’s my mother.”

I remember the conversation clearly. He told me his business acquisitions manager had laughed about how he had ripped Pauline off on the deal. He told me he fired him for it, saying, “We don’t treat people like that around here,” and sent him packing back to England.

He then told me to write the idea up as a formal proposal for him.

The opportunity nearly went in a completely different direction. Before I completed that proposal, another path opened first.

Christchurch: useful commercial testing ground, not the origin of 0199

Before I completed that investor proposal, I ended up in front of Andrew Beaven and Larry Springer and became involved with their Christchurch online-directory venture, which was fronted publicly by Simon Barnett. I was contracted to handle marketing and sales.

Online business directories were commonplace by then. Their product was an online-only directory: businesses paid to appear in a web database. There was no memorable free-calling number, no inbound telephone directory-assistance service and no national call-centre operation connecting callers to businesses.

That is the distinction that matters. I was not trying to invent another website full of business listings. One Number had already shown me the telephone side of the opportunity, and my own sales career had shown me how to build the acquisition engine around it. What I saw was something far more ambitious: a New Zealand-owned telephone directory-assistance service built to take Yellow 018 head on, backed by business data, a high-performance outbound sales team and live inbound operators.

The idea was the phone directory, not another online directory.

The opportunity I saw was a memorable number that New Zealanders could actually call for business information, with real operators answering, a local call centre, and paying businesses connected to genuine consumer demand.

Yellow’s directory-assistance work had been moved offshore. I wanted to bring that work, and the service itself, back home.

The Christchurch venture became a useful commercial testing ground for me because I could apply the sales systems I had developed to prospecting, scripts, pricing, close rates, invoicing and recurring business relationships. But it was not the source of the telephone-directory model that became 0199.

Then the Christchurch earthquakes changed the economics

The earthquake period shattered the environment in which that operation was working. Christchurch businesses were themselves under enormous pressure. Beaven and Springer decided to halt operations.

I could not afford to stop. The revenue base I had expected disappeared at the same time the central-city environment around which much of my Christchurch life had revolved was torn apart.

I left Christchurch and went to Wellington because I needed to keep moving.

The number almost killed 0199 before launch

The four-digit number has its own story. For roughly ten months I was trying to secure a free-to-caller shortcode through Telecom. I kept being told it was being worked on, so I kept moving. The launch was being built around that number. My sales team was already selling a pre-launch offer and taking card payments.

The mission mattered to us. Yellow’s 018 and 0172 directory-assistance work had been shifted offshore to Manila, and I wanted 0199 to be a New Zealand-owned free-calling directory challenger with the call centre here.

“You’re not getting a free calling number.”

By the time the planned launch date had already passed, Telecom finally called me and said: “You’re not getting a free calling number.” Then they hung up almost immediately.

I was standing in the call centre looking at around 15 reps who were full of excitement about launching this thing. My stomach hit the floor. We had sold the pre-launch, taken cards and built the team around a number I had repeatedly been told was being worked on. I knew that losing it at that point could kill the team’s confidence and potentially kill the company.

After ten months of assurances, the timing felt almost like a setup. I had no proof of a conspiracy, but that was honestly how it felt in the room.

2degrees laughed. Compass understood it.

I called 2degrees. They laughed at the idea. Then I called Compass Communications and reached Jo, the national sales manager, while she was driving.

I gave her the pitch for about five minutes. She got it.

“You should have called us first.”

Jo said: “You should have called us first! I’ll have you a shortcode number by the end of the day.”

She emailed me a list of available numbers. I chose 0199.

A few days later, two Compass engineers arrived in Wellington from Auckland carrying the VOIP equipment for the call centre. Within about three days they had the system installed and running.

Ten months of waiting became days of execution. Compass did not just allocate a number. They helped turn the number into a working telecommunications platform.

That was how 0199 became both the brand and the live service.

Looking back, I came to think Jo may have seen more commercial potential in the traffic than even I did at the time. The calls were reverse-charged, effectively the same basic idea as an 0800 or 0508 number: free to the caller, with the receiving business paying for the call. Telcos competed hard for free-calling business accounts. A national directory designed to generate call volume was not just a quirky shortcode. It was potentially a valuable stream of telecommunications traffic.

0199 was not just a memorable number. It was traffic. The sales proposition created customers; the number created calls; the calls themselves had telecommunications value.

Wellington: 0199 becomes its own system

With the number secured and the VOIP platform live, Wellington is where I took those lessons and built my own operation around 0199. It combined directory assistance, a VOIP call centre, business data, outbound sales and inbound customer contact.

$295 + GST. Close it on card. Cashflow now.

At $295 + GST per successful business sale, the economics were powerful. A properly closed Visa, Amex or Mastercard sale could turn a successful call directly into cashflow instead of leaving an invoice sitting in receivables.

Price objection? Handle it. Then close again.

If someone pushed back on the $295 + GST, one of my favourite lines was: “Mate, we’re talking about the petty cash your secretary currently has to buy her lattes for the week with in her top drawer.”

Then straight back to: “Visa, Amex or Mastercard?”

That was the method. Never wander away from the close. Surface the objection, answer it, close again, and let the silence do its job.

That mattered enormously to a growing operation. Organised business data fed prospects into the call centre, scripts converted them, training improved the team, and credit-card closes could turn successful calls directly into working capital for the next round of growth.

The model was not built around one brilliant caller. The value came from turning sales ability into infrastructure: data feeding prospects into the call centre, scripts converting prospects into customers, training creating more capable salespeople, and the directory creating an ongoing commercial relationship.

Competing with Yellow

I had become increasingly critical of the traditional Yellow-style directory and advertising model. From my perspective, many small businesses were paying far too much for products they did not fully understand while digital alternatives were becoming cheaper, faster and easier to measure.

My sales approach became deliberately educational. What was the business actually paying for? Where did enquiries come from? What did a domain really cost? What did hosting cost? What could a website do? Could the result be measured?

Once business owners understood those questions, the old pricing structure became much harder to defend. 0199 could compete by combining a lower-cost directory proposition with direct communication and increasingly useful digital services.

The directory became a technology business

Customers began asking us for websites, business email and hosting. I expanded development capability and personally signed the first website customer. That sale changed the trajectory again.

I also learned that business data was infrastructure. Names, categories, locations, phone numbers, websites and relationships were not just fields in a directory. Properly organised, they could power discovery, outbound sales, customer service, websites and new products.

Years later, back in Christchurch

Many years later, after I had returned to Christchurch, I ran into Andrew Beaven at church. I tentatively suggested that perhaps we should give the idea another go.

That was when the disagreement surfaced.

Beaven told me he believed I had taken the earlier directory idea to Wellington. I was genuinely shocked and strongly disagreed.

My view has always been that the concepts were materially different. Online directories were commonplace. The distinctive idea behind 0199 was the telephone service. My mother’s One Number had already shown me inbound directory assistance before I knew Beaven or Springer. What I built in Wellington was a free-to-caller phone directory, a shortcode, a New Zealand call centre, business data and a sales operation designed to challenge Yellow 018 directly.

That chronology is why the conversation stayed with me. The Christchurch online directory was part of the journey, but it was not the origin of the 0199 concept.

Why 0199 matters to GRID EATER

GRID EATER is not a random new direction. In many respects it is the modern continuation of the same problem I had been working on for years: understand how people find businesses, remove unnecessary friction and cost, organise the data, build the sales system and own more of the infrastructure.

The lineage: Pauline proved businesses would buy a telephone-directory concept → HDG taught me aggressive closing and repeatable sales systems → TelstraClear proved the sales channel could be redesigned → I recognised what One Number could become at national scale → Christchurch gave me a commercial testing ground, not the core 0199 concept → the earthquake forced a reset → Compass supplied the shortcode and stood up the VOIP platform in days → Wellington produced 0199 → 0199 led to websites, hosting and business data → those threads now converge again in GRID EATER.
