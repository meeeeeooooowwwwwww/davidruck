0199: turning sales ability into infrastructure.

0199 combined a four-digit number, business data, a VOIP call centre, outbound sales and inbound directory assistance. The real story began years earlier with sales systems, my mother Pauline Ruck’s directory idea, a Christchurch directory venture, an eleventh-hour fight to secure the number itself and the earthquake that forced a reset.

The sales system came first

My call-centre career did not begin with 0199. Hotel Dynamics Group taught me scripting, qualification and objection handling, but the most valuable lesson was the close.

“Visa, Amex or Mastercard?”

The first close was not supposed to end the conversation. It was designed to force the real objection into the open.

Close → objection → handle it → close again.

“Email me.” “I need to talk to my wife.” “Call me next week.” Price. Timing. Trust. Most objections were familiar and had a response. You handled the objection and went straight back to the same confident close: “Visa, Amex or Mastercard?”

Then you shut up. No rescuing the customer from the silence. They either gave you the card digits, produced another objection, or hung up. If another objection came, you handled it and closed again.

That was the HDG discipline I carried forward. The close was not a polite question saved for the end. It was a diagnostic tool that exposed what was actually stopping the sale, one objection at a time.

I then took that framework into TelstraClear and proved to myself that an entrenched sales-channel assumption could be wrong. A properly designed telephone process could sell products the organisation believed required expensive face-to-face selling.

The lesson I carried forward: sales performance is not just individual talent. It can be turned into scripts, training, process, data and infrastructure.

The directory spark came from Pauline Ruck

My mother, Pauline Ruck, was experimenting with affiliate marketing and online business and developed a directory concept combining telephone access with an online database.

At first I did not fully understand the commercial potential. Then it clicked: a directory was not merely a list. It connected consumer intent with businesses, and that relationship itself could be monetised.

Pauline had already taken her concept onto national television on Dragons’ Den. The Dragons did not invest, but the appearance matters historically because it predates my later involvement with the Christchurch directory venture.

Years later Andrew Beaven accused me of taking the directory idea from the Christchurch venture associated with him and Larry Springer. I reject that account. My mother was publicly presenting her own directory concept before I knew either of them, and her work was what first made the model click for me.

Christchurch: building the acquisition machine

Once I understood the model, I looked for backing and became involved with a Christchurch online-directory venture associated with Andrew Beaven and Larry Springer and fronted publicly by Simon Barnett. I was contracted to handle marketing and sales.

This was where the thinking moved beyond making individual sales. I was building a repeatable acquisition operation around a directory: prospecting, scripts, pricing, close rates, invoicing and recurring business relationships.

Then the Christchurch earthquakes changed the economics

The earthquake period shattered the environment in which that operation was working. Christchurch businesses were themselves under enormous pressure. In my account, the directory owners decided not to enforce invoicing against Christchurch customers we had sold during that period.

I understand the human reason for that decision. Commercially, it removed the revenue base I had expected from the sales operation. At the same time, the central-city environment around which much of my Christchurch life had revolved had been torn apart.

I left Christchurch and went first to Wellington.

The number almost killed 0199 before launch

The four-digit number has its own story. For roughly ten months I was trying to secure a free-to-caller shortcode through Telecom. I kept being told it was being worked on, so I kept moving. The launch was being built around that number. My sales team was already selling a pre-launch offer and taking card payments.

The mission mattered to us. Yellow’s 018 and 0172 directory-assistance work had been shifted offshore to Manila, and I wanted 0199 to be a New Zealand-owned free-calling directory challenger with the call centre here.

“You’re not getting a free calling number.”

By the time the planned launch date had already passed, Telecom finally called me. The message was essentially: “You’re not getting a free calling number.” Then the call ended almost immediately.

I was standing in the call centre looking at around 15 reps who were full of excitement about launching this thing. My stomach hit the floor. We had sold the pre-launch, taken cards and built the team around a number I had repeatedly been told was being worked on. I knew that losing it at that point could kill the team’s confidence and potentially kill the company.

After ten months of assurances, the timing felt almost like a setup. I had no proof of a conspiracy, but that was honestly how it felt in the room.

2degrees laughed. Compass understood it.

I started calling other carriers. My recollection is that 2degrees laughed at the idea. Then I called Compass Communications and reached Jo, the national sales manager, while she was driving.

I gave her the pitch for about five minutes. She got it.

“You should have called us first.”

Jo’s response was essentially: “You should have called us first. I’ll have you a shortcode number by the end of the day.”

And she did.

That was how 0199 got the number that became the brand.

Looking back, I came to think Jo may have seen more commercial potential in the traffic than even I did at the time. The calls were reverse-charged, effectively the same basic idea as an 0800 or 0508 number: free to the caller, with the receiving business paying for the call. Telcos competed hard for free-calling business accounts. A national directory designed to generate call volume was not just a quirky shortcode. It was potentially a valuable stream of telecommunications traffic.

0199 was not just a memorable number. It was traffic. The sales proposition created customers; the number created calls; the calls themselves had telecommunications value.

Wellington: 0199 becomes its own system

With the number secured, Wellington is where I took those lessons and built my own operation around 0199. It combined directory assistance, a VOIP call centre, business data, outbound sales and inbound customer contact.

$295 + GST. Close it on card. Cashflow now.

At $295 + GST per successful business sale, the economics were powerful. A properly closed Visa, Amex or Mastercard sale could turn a successful call directly into cashflow instead of leaving an invoice sitting in receivables.

Price objection? Handle it. Then close again.

If someone pushed back on the $295 + GST, one of my favourite lines was: “Mate, we’re talking about the petty cash your secretary currently has to buy her lattes for the week with in her top drawer.”

Then straight back to: “Visa, Amex or Mastercard?”

That was the method. Never wander away from the close. Surface the objection, answer it, close again, and let the silence do its job.

That mattered enormously to a growing operation. Organised business data fed prospects into the call centre, scripts converted them, training improved the team, and credit-card closes could turn successful calls directly into working capital for the next round of growth.

The model was not built around one brilliant caller. The value came from turning sales ability into infrastructure: data feeding prospects into the call centre, scripts converting prospects into customers, training creating more capable salespeople, and the directory creating an ongoing commercial relationship.

Competing with Yellow

I had become increasingly critical of the traditional Yellow-style directory and advertising model. From my perspective, many small businesses were paying far too much for products they did not fully understand while digital alternatives were becoming cheaper, faster and easier to measure.

My sales approach became deliberately educational. What was the business actually paying for? Where did enquiries come from? What did a domain really cost? What did hosting cost? What could a website do? Could the result be measured?

Once business owners understood those questions, the old pricing structure became much harder to defend. 0199 could compete by combining a lower-cost directory proposition with direct communication and increasingly useful digital services.

The directory became a technology business

Customers began asking us for websites, business email and hosting. I expanded development capability and personally signed the first website customer. That sale changed the trajectory again.

I also learned that business data was infrastructure. Names, categories, locations, phone numbers, websites and relationships were not just fields in a directory. Properly organised, they could power discovery, outbound sales, customer service, websites and new products.

Why 0199 matters to GRID EATER

GRID EATER is not a random new direction. In many respects it is the modern continuation of the same problem I had been working on for years: understand how people find businesses, remove unnecessary friction and cost, organise the data, build the sales system and own more of the infrastructure.

The lineage: HDG taught me sales systems → TelstraClear proved the channel could be redesigned → Pauline Ruck provided the directory spark → Christchurch became the commercial testing ground → the earthquake forced a reset → Compass delivered the 0199 shortcode when the launch was in danger → Wellington produced 0199 → 0199 led to websites, hosting and business data → those threads now converge again in GRID EATER.
