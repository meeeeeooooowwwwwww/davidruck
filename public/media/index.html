Media & Culture

From choir and bands to DJing, production and underground culture.

Music was one of the first places I learned to build something around my own judgement. Choir became drums and bands, bands became DJing and production, and that eventually turned into parties, Surreal, radio and television.

Music started before I was old enough to understand careers.

I joined the Treble Boys’ Choir at about four years old while attending St Andrew’s College ↗ in Christchurch, under choir master Clifton Cook. I stayed involved until about age twelve, when I left Christchurch for boarding school at Timaru Boys’ High School ↗.

That move changed the music around me. I started drumming, became much more interested in rock and spent my teenage years trying to form bands. Nirvana was a huge influence. I was much more grunge-skater kid than polished musician.

I genuinely wanted a band, but getting two or three other people equally motivated, rehearsed and actually in the room was another problem entirely. That frustration is one of the reasons DJing made so much sense to me. I could spend hours finding extraordinary music, prepare it properly and then play it to people without depending on the rest of a band being ready.

At roughly the same age, another thread had already started. During a family trip to England, my cousins introduced me to electronic dance music. I was also heavily influenced by 1980s hip hop. Later, the darker end of electronic music grabbed me much more than polished mainstream dance: harder electro was probably the closest thing to my natural DJ identity, while artists and labels around the harder drum & bass world, including Panacea, Metalheadz and Usual Suspects, shaped what I listened for. When dubstep emerged, the heavier and more intelligently engineered end of it appealed to me for the same reason.

Production: I usually kept it to myself.

I was always interested in making music as well as playing it. The problem was that I almost never liked anything I produced enough to release it. I would finish something, hear every flaw in it and keep it to myself. That is still largely true today.

One exception started as a technical exercise. I wanted to practise time-stretching a vocal across a completely different genre, so I built a drum & bass remix around an Eminem vocal. It was not even the style I was most identified with as a DJ and I did not regard the track as some grand artistic statement.

Other people reacted very differently. The remix accumulated hundreds of thousands of listens on SoundCloud in my recollection, which eventually convinced me to put it on YouTube. The upload has since passed 35,000 views. I no longer have access to that old YouTube/Gmail account, but the upload is still there.

That became another lesson I kept seeing elsewhere: being close to your own work can make you a terrible judge of what the market will value. Release, observe, measure, then listen to the response.

Nelson: the parties started while I was still working in kitchens.

By about eighteen I had started DJing. At the time I was working in hospitality in Nelson, including at the Boat Shed Cafe, and I would sometimes pick up extra shifts at the fish factory down at the port for more cash.

The DJing quickly turned into parties at the house. We would bring in local bands and DJs, decorate the place completely differently each time, then walk around town handing out flyers. It was extremely low-tech promotion, but it worked.

The first few parties became popular enough that someone finally asked the obvious question: why are you not charging people? I genuinely had not considered it.

Alien Strike Zone

The third big house party was called Alien Strike Zone. About a hundred people turned up.

I spent roughly $100 on decorations and convinced local businesses to give or lend me materials we could repurpose. We covered the inside and outside of the house, wrapped the gardens in white sheeting we had found, built a crashed UFO, used fluorescent paint over the flowers and lit everything with UV lights. The whole property looked like it had been infested by aliens.

Instead of charging admission, I put a voluntary gold-coin donation at the door. We collected about $200. At eighteen, that felt like a jackpot.

The bigger moment was not the money. Murray, founder of The Gathering, brought his DJ equipment and several high-profile Gathering DJs to play. They were impressed by both the turnout and how far we had pushed the decor on almost no budget.

That party was probably the first clear proof to me that I could create an experience, convince people to show up, make almost nothing look substantial and build an audience through sheer promotion and energy.

Back in Christchurch, the house-party instinct became Surreal.

Surreal was a brand, not a formally constituted company. It was simply the name I put around a run of underground rave parties in Christchurch.

The parties themselves were illegal in the sense that we were taking over empty buildings and running large, unlicensed all-night events. That was the era. Looking back, it feels like the tail end of a period when young people could still get away with doing things that would be shut down almost instantly now.

The promotion was completely physical. Flyers, streets, schools, record shops and word of mouth. I learned very quickly that the right people could move information through social networks much faster than I could alone, so I recruited outgoing young women I met around town to help distribute flyers through local high-school circles and nightlife networks.

The parties got bigger, the buildings got larger and the production became more serious. Better sound, lighting, decor, security, suppliers and paid admission followed. There was no boardroom, office or formal structure behind Surreal. It was a name, a reputation and a rapidly growing crowd.

The night the police let the rave continue

At one Christchurch event, more than 2,000 kids were inside when uniformed police on the beat found the party. I was sweating. There was every chance they were about to shut the entire thing down.

Instead, one of them said: “We were wondering where all the kids were tonight.”

They looked around, saw that the event appeared to be under control and said: “Well, looks like you have everything under control.”

Then they left us to it.

We carried on until about 6am. It is one of those stories that captures the time better than almost anything else. The party was enormous, the police knew exactly what they had found, and rather than treat it as a crisis they recognised that someone was at least making an effort to give the kids somewhere to go.

The pattern started here: create a strong identity, make something visually memorable, recruit people who can spread it, then build enough organisation behind the audience to stop the whole thing collapsing. I would later recognise the same pattern in sales organisations, websites, directories and digital businesses.

PulzarFM After Dark: I pushed the station much harder.

At Christchurch independent dance station PulzarFM ↗ I worked as Sales Manager and also took over the late Friday- and Saturday-night airwaves. I called the shows After Dark, deliberately carrying the name forward from my ALT TV years.

Although I loved selected hard drum & bass and later dubstep, I was primarily a harder-edge electro DJ at the time. Friday became harder electro and harder house from about 10pm until dawn. Saturday was deliberately different: harder dubstep and drum & bass, pushed as far as I could find it. Across both nights I wanted weight, darkness, clever engineering and a sound that did not feel interchangeable with daytime programming.

I also opened the studio up. Guest DJs could come in and play live, including people who were outside the established networks. Christchurch already had a long-running alternative institution in RDU ↗, while Galaxy Records ↗ was one of the city’s key music-scene hubs. I was much less interested in protecting an existing gate than in finding out what happened when you threw it open.

The response was immediate. In my recollection, the Friday and Saturday late shows became the strongest shows on the station by a wide margin. They created noise in the Christchurch dance scene precisely because the sound and access were different.

“You know we have a predominantly female audience, right?”

Station founder Andy Poulsen and I argued about the direction. He pushed back on the music, the harder tone and things I said on air. At one point he told me: “You know we have a predominantly female audience, right?”

I told him I thought the opposite was true: the audience was predominantly young male. A few days later he came back and told me he had discovered I was right.

The surviving public profile of PulzarFM later described its target audience as ages 16–35 with a 75/25 male/female split. That matches the audience profile I had argued for inside the station.

Disruption was never just a slogan for me. Surreal challenged how events were promoted. TelstraClear challenged an expensive sales assumption. Pulzar After Dark challenged the station’s idea of its own audience and opened the booth to people outside the usual channels. The recurring pattern was the same: question the accepted model, watch the audience, then push harder when the evidence says the old assumption is wrong.

Funk & Fish & Chips

I became involved in repositioning a struggling takeaway as a colourful late-night destination built around strong fast food, arcade games, street art, rave music and a DJ setup. The earthquake ended the business, but the experience reinforced how much identity and positioning matter.

My first move to Auckland: HRV and ALT TV happened at the same time.

I have moved to Auckland twice in my life. The first time was because I had fallen madly in love with a woman I met in online chat rooms. I moved north to be with her and started looking for work.

I initially took a job at Compass Communications ↗, but it was brief. I quickly found the opportunity at HRV ↗, which became the beginning of the HRV sales-management chapter of my career.

While I was working at HRV I met Jack, a presenter at ALT TV. He asked me to produce the station’s late Friday- and Saturday-night shows. I took them on and rebranded the late-night block as ALT After Dark.

So ALT TV was not a later career phase after HRV. The two overlapped. During the week I was immersed in sales systems and outbound performance at HRV; on Friday and Saturday nights I was producing alternative music television.

ALT TV ↗ itself was created by Thane Kirby and Ricky Newby. Kirby was also one of the founders of George FM ↗, which made ALT TV part of the same Auckland alternative and electronic-music culture I had been drawn to for years.

Surreal predates ALT TV by years. ALT TV was a later Auckland chapter running in parallel with HRV, while Surreal’s origins reached back to the Nelson house-party years.
