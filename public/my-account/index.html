My Account of the Public Record

For years, other people have had the loudest pages about my name. This is the long version from me: the businesses I built, the systems I challenged, the Pākehā Party, the backlash I experienced, an old conviction repeatedly revived in search results, and what I learned from all of it.

Editorial note: This page is deliberately first person. Where I describe motives, pressures, hostility or institutional behaviour, I am describing my own experience and interpretation. I distinguish that from facts I can independently anchor.

Why I am writing this

Search engines are very good at preserving controversy and very bad at preserving proportion. A headline written during a few chaotic weeks can sit above twenty or thirty years of somebody's life forever. That has happened to me.

I am not pretending I never made mistakes. I did. I am not pretending every criticism of me was dishonest. It was not. But I reject the idea that the most sensational headline, the shortest quote or somebody else's political interpretation should become my permanent biography.

This page exists because I have a right to speak for myself.

Before politics: 0199 and challenging Yellow

Long before the Pākehā Party, I had already developed a habit of walking directly into markets dominated by much larger incumbents.

After the Christchurch earthquake I moved to Wellington and built 0199 Directory Assistance around a four-digit telephone number and a VOIP call-centre operation. The original idea was to compete with established directory assistance. It quickly became bigger than that.

At the time I believed the traditional Yellow-style directory and advertising model had become far too expensive and offered poor value to many small New Zealand businesses. I sometimes described it in much blunter language. What I meant was that I was meeting business owners who were paying significant amounts for products they did not always understand while cheaper and increasingly measurable digital alternatives were emerging.

My approach in the sales and consulting phase was to educate first: what did a listing actually do, where were enquiries coming from, what could a website do, what did a domain cost, what did hosting cost, and how could a business measure whether advertising was producing customers?

0199 did not have Yellow's history, capital or market position. That was exactly the point. I wanted to show that a much smaller operator could use telecommunications, data, direct sales and the web to offer businesses an alternative.

Customers began asking us for websites, email and hosting. I expanded development capability and signed the first website customer myself. That changed my career.

2013: the Pākehā Party

In June 2013 I created a Facebook page called the Pākehā Party. It began partly as provocation and partly as a simple political point about what I saw as unequal treatment based on ethnicity. I was not an established politician and I did not have a party machine behind me.

Then it exploded.

The scale: Contemporary 2013 reporting recorded the page moving from roughly 4,000 likes around its first major television exposure on 8 July to more than 50,000 within days, surpassing mainstream New Zealand political parties in Facebook popularity at that point. Likes are not votes, but the speed and scale of the audience were extraordinary for an unaffiliated page created only weeks earlier.

I remember TVNZ's coverage being the moment I first understood the comparison with established parties. Whatever somebody thinks of the message, a page created by an unknown businessman had suddenly generated a larger social-media audience than organisations that had existed for decades.

That was when the atmosphere changed.

What I believed I was saying

My argument was about equal treatment under one set of rules. Critics saw the page as racist, inflammatory or historically ignorant. Supporters saw it as permission to say things they felt could no longer be said in mainstream politics. Those disagreements were real, and I am not rewriting them away.

I also publicly rejected racist abuse appearing around the page and began moderating comments. A mass Facebook audience is not a disciplined political organisation. It attracts people for completely different reasons, including people whose views I did not share and did not want attached to me.

The speed created a different kind of threat

My interpretation, from inside the event, was that the issue stopped being merely whether people agreed with me. The rapid growth itself became threatening to established political and media structures. An outsider had demonstrated that one provocative message could bypass normal gatekeepers and gather a national audience almost overnight.

I drew hostility from multiple and often mutually opposed political camps. That was one of the strangest aspects of it: people who disagreed with each other about almost everything could still agree that I was a problem.

I also experienced hostility from people I understood to have gang connections and from street-level supporters associated, in my view, with different political causes. I am not claiming that every critic coordinated with every other critic, nor that I can prove who was acting at anyone else's direction. I am describing what the environment felt like from my position inside it.

What I mean by an establishment machine

I do not mean that a secret committee met in a room and decided what everybody should do to David Ruck.

I mean something more ordinary and, in some ways, more powerful. Political incentives, media incentives, party interests, activist networks, social pressure and reputational fear can all push in the same direction without central coordination. A person can become a symbol. Once that happens, every old mistake becomes newly relevant, every clumsy sentence becomes evidence of character, and every hostile interpretation is repeated until it feels like settled biography.

That was my experience of 2013.

About the “criminal background” headline

One of the search results that has followed my name for years is an NZ Herald headline from the Pākehā Party controversy saying that I “admit[ted] criminal background”. The words are connected to something real. The impression they leave without chronology is, in my view, deeply misleading.

The offending was from the 1990s. Contemporary reporting in 2013 itself described it as about sixteen years old. It is now nearly three decades in the past.

I do not deny the theft. I pleaded guilty. I served the sentence. I regret it.

What I dispute is the way an old offence was revived during a political controversy and compressed into a permanent search-engine identity, including a reported valuation of $40,000 that I do not accept reflects what I actually took.

The context behind the offence

There was a reason I committed that offence. That is not the same thing as an excuse.

I was young and heavily involved in Christchurch's rave and events scene. I had organised a large rave that, by my recollection, attracted roughly 3,000 people. It was a major undertaking involving a venue, production, security, ticket sales and a lot of people relying on the event going ahead.

The venue and security arrangements became problematic. By the time I understood how serious the situation was, the event was already being set up and tickets had been sold. Things happened around and after that event that I am not presently prepared to put into a public accusation against other people. What I can say is that property went missing and I felt responsible for trying to fix the losses.

I made a stupid and unlawful decision about how to do that.

I took DJ equipment belonging to somebody I knew. At the time I rationalised it by telling myself that the equipment was insured, that the owner would get replacements, and that I could use what I took to replace some of what had gone missing after the rave.

That reasoning was self-serving and wrong. Insurance does not make taking somebody else's property acceptable. The owner should never have been made responsible for a problem that was mine.

The $40,000 figure

My recollection is that the property consisted of two second-hand turntables and several boxes of second-hand vinyl records. I do not believe that property was worth anything approaching $40,000.

I have wondered how that valuation arose, including whether insurance considerations played a part, but I cannot prove another person's motive and I am not going to turn speculation into an accusation.

I also did not appreciate, when I entered the guilty plea, that the valuation was embedded in what I was admitting. By the time I realised the significance of it I was standing before the judge. I felt guilty about the theft itself, wanted to take responsibility and wanted the matter over. I pleaded guilty.

I accept that I stole the equipment and I accept responsibility for that. I do not accept that my guilty plea should be treated as proof that I personally agreed the equipment was worth $40,000.

What I would change

If I could go back, I would not take the equipment. I would deal with the losses differently, involve the appropriate people and accept whatever financial damage landed on me. I was young, I panicked, I rationalised a bad decision and somebody else paid the price for it.

I cannot undo that. I can only tell the truth about it, including the parts that make me look bad.

What happened afterward

The political controversy affected my reputation, relationships and ability to operate publicly. People who only knew the headlines could arrive at a complete judgement before ever speaking to me.

I also made later statements and choices that created further controversy. I am not going to pretend every consequence in my life was caused by somebody else. Part of rebuilding is recognising where my own anger, judgement or language made situations worse.

At the same time, I will not surrender the other lesson: institutions, media narratives and search engines can freeze a human being at the worst or most controversial moment of their life and keep presenting that moment as though nothing else ever happened.

Why this matters to the work I do now

The experiences of 0199 and 2013 pushed me toward the same conclusion from different directions: independence matters.

I want to own the infrastructure I can own. I want businesses to understand the systems they are paying for. I want technology to reduce dependence on expensive gatekeepers. I want data organised so it can be useful rather than trapped. And I want my own site to be the canonical place where my own history is available in full.

AI has accelerated that dramatically. GRID EATER is where many of those threads are coming back together: hosting, domains, websites, business discovery, data, automation and direct human support.

The point of this page

You do not have to agree with my politics, my interpretation of 2013 or every decision I have made.

I am not asking for that.

I am asking for proportion. I am asking for chronology. I am asking that somebody searching my name can find the entrepreneur who built 0199, the salesman who learned to build systems, the person who worked in restaurants, radio and television, the operator rebuilding around hosting and AI, the man who made an old criminal mistake and accepts it, and the person who experienced a political firestorm from the inside.

A headline can be part of a life. It should not be allowed to become the whole life.
