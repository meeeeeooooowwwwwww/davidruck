0199 Directory Assistance · first-person history

0199 did not begin with me. The spark came from my mother.

The real origin of 0199 runs through Hotel Dynamics Group, a breakthrough selling TelstraClear services by telephone, my mother Pauline Ruck’s early directory and affiliate-marketing work, a Christchurch directory venture, the earthquakes and finally Wellington. By the time I launched 0199, years of sales systems, directory thinking and business relationships had already converged.

First came Hotel Dynamics Group

My call-centre career did not begin with 0199. One of the foundations was Hotel Dynamics Group, where I learned direct and telephone sales at a very high level. I became obsessed with the mechanics of conversion: opening lines, tone, qualification, objection handling, offer construction, closing, training and measurement.

That experience taught me that telephone selling was not simply a matter of putting people on phones and hoping for results. It could be engineered as a repeatable system.

TelstraClear: the point where the system proved itself

While I was working in telecommunications, TelstraClear brought me in to apply what I had learned at HDG to its own direct-sales environment. My understanding at the time was that the company regarded many of its consumer products as fundamentally face-to-face products, sold through expensive door-to-door operations.

I disagreed. I believed the same products could be sold efficiently over the telephone if the offer, script and sales process were right.

I remember the national sales manager coming into the office and laughing at the idea that I would successfully sell the product by phone. At the time I was simply confused by the reaction. Later I understood the organisational context differently: Telstra had invested heavily in field-sales infrastructure, while I was proposing that a telephone and a disciplined sales process could do work that previously required representatives, transport, territory management and substantial logistics.

I did not fail. I excelled. My conversion rate on suitable prospects climbed extraordinarily high, at times approaching 100 percent. What mattered to me was not simply that I could sell. It was proof that an entrenched assumption about the sales channel could be wrong.

TelstraClear subsequently gave me permission to offer broadband without a fixed-term contract. To my knowledge, I made the first sale under what was then New Zealand’s first no-contract broadband offer. The proposition was extremely strong: customers could try the service without being locked into the sort of long commitment they expected from telecommunications companies.

The lesson I carried forward: a large incumbent can spend enormous amounts building a sales model around an assumption. A smaller operator can sometimes overturn that assumption simply by understanding the customer, changing the offer and measuring the result.

The idea that became 0199 came from Pauline Ruck

At roughly the same stage of my life, my mother, Pauline Ruck, was becoming involved in affiliate marketing and online business. She had developed a directory concept that combined a telephone service with an online database.

At first I did not fully understand what she was doing. Then, a few months later, it clicked. I suddenly understood the commercial logic: a directory was not merely a book or a list. It was a way of connecting consumer intent with businesses, and the relationship itself could be monetised.

Pauline had already taken her concept onto national television on Dragons’ Den. The Dragons did not invest, and I do not think she presented the opportunity as strongly as it could have been presented. But the appearance matters historically because it establishes that the idea existed in my family before I later met the Christchurch businesspeople with whom I became involved in another directory operation.

That clip is important to me for another reason. Years later, after 0199 existed, Andrew Beaven accused me of taking the directory idea from the Christchurch venture associated with him and Larry Springer. I reject that account completely. The chronology is straightforward from my perspective: my mother was publicly presenting her own directory concept before I knew Andrew Beaven or Larry Springer, and it was her work that first caused the directory model to click for me.

A directory itself was hardly a unique invention. Directories existed everywhere. What became distinctive about 0199 was the scale, the telephone sales model, the pricing, the four-digit number, the call-centre infrastructure, the business data and the later transition into websites, hosting and digital services.

Looking for backing in Christchurch

Once the model clicked for me, I started looking for investors and people with the resources to build something larger. That eventually brought me into contact with established Christchurch business figures Andrew Beaven and Larry Springer, with Simon Barnett fronting the directory venture publicly.

Pauline’s concept had included telephone access backed by an online database. The Christchurch directory I became involved with was, in my recollection, primarily online. I was contracted to handle marketing and sales.

This was where the sales machinery I had been developing became useful. I was no longer just selling a product personally. I was thinking about how to build a repeatable acquisition operation around a directory: prospecting, scripting, pricing, close rates, invoicing and recurring business relationships.

Then the Christchurch earthquakes changed everything

The earthquake period shattered the environment in which that business was operating. Christchurch businesses were themselves under enormous pressure. In my account, the directory owners decided not to enforce invoicing against Christchurch customers we had sold during that period.

I understand the human reason for that decision. Commercially, however, it destroyed the revenue base I had expected from the sales operation. At the same time I had been pushed out of the central-city environment around which much of my Christchurch life had revolved.

I left Christchurch and went first to Wellington.

Wellington: 0199 becomes its own system

Wellington is where I took the lessons and built my own operation around the four-digit number 0199. It combined directory assistance, a VOIP call centre, business data, outbound sales and inbound customer contact.

Every successful business sale for 0199 was worth about $295 + GST. That is one reason the old Dragons’ Den clip of my mother is so striking to me today. The concept the Dragons dismissed could become a product where a single completed telephone sale generated hundreds of dollars in revenue, and the model could be repeated by a trained sales team.

The economics were not based on one brilliant call. They came from turning sales ability into infrastructure: data feeding prospects into a call centre, scripts converting prospects into customers, training creating more capable salespeople and a directory creating an ongoing relationship with the business.

Competing with Yellow

By then I had become increasingly critical of the traditional Yellow-style directory and advertising model. From my perspective, many small businesses were paying far too much for products they did not fully understand while digital alternatives were becoming cheaper, faster and easier to measure.

My sales approach became deliberately educational. What was the business actually paying for? Where did enquiries come from? What did a domain really cost? What did hosting cost? What could a website do? Could results be measured? Once a business owner understood those questions, the old pricing model became much harder to defend.

0199 could compete because it combined a lower-cost directory proposition with direct communication and, increasingly, digital services.

The directory became a technology business

Customers began asking us for websites, business email and hosting. I expanded development capability and personally signed the first website customer. That sale changed the trajectory again.

I also learned that business data was infrastructure. Names, categories, locations, phone numbers, websites and relationships were not just fields in a directory. Properly organised, they could power discovery, outbound sales, customer service, websites and later entirely new products.

Why this history matters to GRID EATER

GRID EATER is not a random new direction for me. In many respects it is the modern version of the same obsession that ran through HDG, TelstraClear, Pauline’s directory concept and 0199: understand how people find businesses, remove unnecessary friction and cost, organise the data, build the sales system and own more of the infrastructure.

The lineage: HDG taught me sales → TelstraClear proved the channel could be redesigned → Pauline Ruck gave me the directory spark → Christchurch gave me a commercial testing ground → the earthquake forced a reset → Wellington produced 0199 → 0199 led to websites, hosting and business data → those threads now converge again in GRID EATER.
